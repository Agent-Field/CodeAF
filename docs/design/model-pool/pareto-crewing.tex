\title{\textbf{One Dial}\\[6pt]
\large Learning cost-aware model assignment for multi-role agent harnesses\\
from graded outcomes}
\author{Santosh Kumar Radha}
\affil{AgentField AI}
\date{17 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\thispagestyle{fancy}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A multi-role agent harness must decide, before each task, which language model occupies
which role: which one writes, which one reviews, which one plans. We give a
decision-theoretic account of that assignment problem and a learner for it whose only
user-facing parameter is a scalar $L\ge0$, the dollar cost of a failed task. Every other
quantity --- prices, token volumes, acceptance probabilities, the weight of a published
catalog, the reliability of each grading source --- is measured or learned. The assignment
minimises $J(a;x,L)=\E[B(a,x)]+L\,(1-P(g{=}1\mid a,x))$, where $g$ is an executable,
model-free grade on the artefact the run leaves behind. We characterise exactly what the
dial reaches: sweeping $L$ over $[0,\infty)$ traces the exposed vertices of the lower
convex hull of the achievable (bill, failure) set and nothing else, so Pareto-optimal
assignments strictly inside the hull are selected by no stake. We prove that the
scale-free knee used by a standard baseline is always such a vertex, and retire it anyway,
because its implied stake is covariant with the price scale while a user's stake is not.
Around that core we give a per-role additive-logit quality model with semi-bandit
feedback, a hierarchical catalog-anchored prior with a cap derived from a between-site
random effect, sample complexity in closed form, drift by variance inflation, a cascade
break-even condition, and a switching rule with an anytime-valid test. We evaluate on
$202$ graded runs of one production harness, $132$ of them carrying a per-role token
split. Three results. The measured role shape is nothing like the fixed one: the writing
role takes a median $0.97$ of a run's tokens but only $0.52$ of its dollars, while the
reviewing role takes a median $0.026$ of the tokens and $0.44$ of the dollars, with a tail
reaching $0.95$ and nine runs in $132$ on which the role that only reads the work takes
more than half the tokens; learning that shape --- shrunk on the log scale, which matters --- cuts
leave-one-out bill prediction error from $302\%$ to $58\%$ and the median error factor
from $3.4\times$ to $1.6\times$. In a hindsight check over the twelve assignments run at
least three times, the learned rule's pick at $L{=}\$1$ costs $3.4\%$ more than the
best assignment in hindsight, against $57.5\%$ for the same rule priced with a fixed
shape; at $L{=}\$10$ it costs $92\%$ more, and we show why --- the additive model cannot
represent the role-pair interaction that the hindsight winner exploits. Replaying the
ledger in time order, the rule's empirical regret at $L{=}\$1$ flattens from
$\$0.90$ to $\$0.027$ per task across thirds while a fixed-shape variant stays at
$\$0.45$; at $L{=}\$10$ it does not flatten at all, which is what a stake of ten dollars
and $136$ graded runs are jointly unable to resolve. We report the negative results
in full.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} model selection, decision theory, combinatorial semi-bandits,
Thompson sampling, cascades, empirical Bayes, cost-aware learning, LLM agents.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An agent harness that drives language models through software tasks makes a structured
choice before every task: which model occupies which role. Contemporary harnesses run two
to five roles --- one that writes the change, one that reviews it, one that decomposes the
brief, sometimes one that grades and one that converses --- and the published model
catalog offers hundreds of candidates for each. The choice matters twice over. It sets the
bill, which varies by two orders of magnitude across feasible assignments, and it sets the
probability that the task comes back done, which varies by tens of percentage points. The
two move together, and the rate at which a practitioner is willing to trade one for the
other is not a property of the system.

The information available to make the choice arrives in three units and on three
timescales. A published catalog gives a price and one or more benchmark indexes for every
model on the day it appears --- in the snapshot we use, $522$ models, $417$ of them
priced. A site's own past runs are few, but they are drawn from exactly the distribution
that matters. Other sites' runs are many, but they are drawn from other people's
repositories. A usable rule must combine all three, and must keep working on the day a
model nobody has run appears in the feed.

\paragraph{The thesis.}
We argue for a one-parameter decision rule. The practitioner states one number: $L$, the
dollars a failed task costs them. Everything else --- the per-role token volumes, the
prices, the acceptance probabilities, the weight the catalog deserves, the reliability of
each source of grades --- is measured from graded runs or read from a public feed. The
rule minimises expected loss
\[
J(a;x,L)=\E[B(a,x)]+L\,\big(1-P(g{=}1\mid a,x)\big),
\]
its reachable set is exactly the exposed lower convex hull of the achievable (bill,
failure) set, and as graded runs accumulate its picks converge to the assignment that
minimises realised loss. The units are dollars throughout. There is no utility scale, no
temperature, and no exchange rate the practitioner does not set.

\paragraph{Why one dial and not zero.}
A rule with no dial must choose the trade-off on the practitioner's behalf, and the usual
device for that is a scale-free knee: the point on the cost--quality front farthest above
the chord in $(\log\text{cost},\,\text{quality})$. We show in Section~\ref{sec:decision}
that such a knee is always a hull vertex, hence always the answer for \emph{some} stake
--- and that this is exactly the problem, because the stake it implies is never shown, and
it moves when prices move in a direction opposite to the one a fixed stake implies. A rule
with no units cannot be told what a failure costs.

\paragraph{Why one dial and not several.}
Every additional knob --- an exploration rate, a quality floor, a temperature, a price
ceiling --- is a quantity the practitioner must set without a unit in which to reason
about it, and each interacts with the others. We show that the quantities usually exposed
as knobs are either derivable from $L$ and the measured cells (the exploration budget
prices itself inside $J$; the switching threshold is a cache loss with a receipt) or
estimable from data (the catalog's weight, the between-site variance, the drift rate, each
a moment estimator).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item A decision-theoretic formulation of role assignment with a single scalar in dollars,
and a complete characterisation of what sweeping it can and cannot reach
(Propositions~\ref{prop:hull}--\ref{prop:sweep}), together with a proof that the
scale-free knee is always reachable (Proposition~\ref{prop:kneehull}) and the scale
argument for retiring it anyway (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}).
\item An executable, model-free reward, and a derivation of what a noisy proxy for it is
worth in units of graded runs (Proposition~\ref{prop:reliab}), measured on three candidate
proxies (Section~\ref{sec:reward}).
\item A per-role additive-logit quality model with semi-bandit feedback, a hierarchical
catalog-anchored prior whose external evidence is capped by a between-site random effect
(Proposition~\ref{prop:post}), closed-form sample complexity
(Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}), and drift by variance inflation
(Section~\ref{sec:quality}).
\item A learned per-(role, model) token shape in place of a fixed one, and the observation
--- which turns out to carry the entire effect --- that the shape must be shrunk on the
log scale (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\item Cascades, exploration under a hard budget, switching under an anytime-valid test,
and pooling across sites, each as a separate mechanism with its own break-even condition
and its own stated limits (Sections~\ref{sec:decide}--\ref{sec:federation}).
\item An evaluation on $202$ graded runs of a production harness --- a cost-model
experiment, a hindsight check against the best assignment at three stakes, and a
convergence replay --- reported with its negative results (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}

\begin{definition}[Roles, models, assignments]
Let $\roles$ be a finite set of \emph{roles} and $\mods$ a finite set of \emph{models}. An
\emph{assignment} is a map $a:\roles\to\mods$ subject to feasibility: each role $r$ carries
a predicate $\mathrm{fit}_r(m)$ (tool use, modalities, context window) and a set
$D\subseteq\roles\times\roles$ of role pairs that may not be held by models of the same
vendor. Write $\acts$ for the feasible set.
\end{definition}

Throughout, $\roles=\{\mathrm{writer},\mathrm{reviewer},\mathrm{planner}\}$: the writer
produces the change, the reviewer reads it without producing one, the planner decomposes
the brief into steps. Nothing below depends on $|\roles|=3$, and nothing depends on those
three being the roles; a harness with a separate grading role or a separate conversational
role adds coordinates to $a$ without changing any statement.

A \emph{task} carries a context $x$ recorded before the run starts: an entry channel
$c\in\chans$ (which interface the work arrived through), a size class $s\in\sizes$, and any
other pre-run covariate. A \emph{run} is one attempt by one assignment on one task, and
leaves behind an \emph{artefact} --- in the software setting, a working tree.

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:today}

We state two baselines precisely, because the contributions below are differences from
them and because both are what deployed systems actually do.

\paragraph{Baseline B0: a fixed token shape and a scale-free knee.}
Quality is read from the catalog alone. A model's score for a role is a weighted,
pool-normalised blend of its published indexes,
\begin{equation}
\hat q_r(m)=100\sum_{i} w_{r,i}\,\frac{\varphi_{m,i}}{\max_{m'\in\mods}\varphi_{m',i}},
\label{eq:catq}
\end{equation}
the maximum taken over the candidate list handed to the call, so the same model scores
differently in a stronger pool; a missing index is imputed from ratios to the indexes the
model does publish. Cost is read from a \emph{fixed shape}: each role carries an
input-to-output ratio, a cache-hit share, and a share of the task's tokens, all of them
constants. The role's unit cost is $(1-\gamma_r)\pi_m+\gamma_r\pi^c_m+\chi_m/\mathrm{InOut}_r$
and the assignment's bill is the volume-weighted sum. Assignments are sorted by bill and
swept for strict improvement in quality, giving the Pareto front $\front$; the chosen
assignment is the member farthest above the chord of the front in $(\ln B,Q)$:
\begin{equation}
\text{knee}=\argmax_{a\in\front}\Big[(Q(a)-Q_0)-(Q_1-Q_0)\frac{\ln B(a)-\ln B_0}{\ln B_1-\ln B_0}\Big].
\label{eq:knee}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Baseline B1: a posterior-mean rule with a catalog prior.}
B1 keeps B0's fixed cost shape but replaces the catalog score with a shrinkage blend of it
and the site's own measurements. A cell supplies a mean $\bar y_{r,m}$ over $N$
observations, and the role's score becomes
\begin{equation}
(1-w)\,\hat q_r(m) + w\,\bar y_{r,m},\qquad w=\frac{N}{N+\kappa},\qquad \kappa=30 .
\label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
This is the posterior mean of a conjugate normal model in which the catalog is a prior
worth $\kappa$ observations; Section~\ref{sec:quality} makes that reading precise and
Proposition~\ref{prop:post} generalises it to three sources.

\paragraph{Four things neither baseline can see.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item[\textbf{G1}] \emph{No exploration.} The pick is the argmin at the posterior mean. A
role--model pair nobody has rated keeps its catalog score forever, and a model outside the
catalog's index coverage keeps whatever default it was given. Section~\ref{sec:decide}
shows this is not merely a theoretical gap: it is the one regime in which the rule's
regret does not fall at all.
\item[\textbf{G2}] \emph{No measured cost.} The token shape is a constant, so the bill of
an assignment is a statement about prices and not about what the models do. In particular
nothing in the shape varies by model: a reviewer that emits ten times as many tokens as
another is priced as though it emitted the same number. Section~\ref{sec:exp} measures
what that omission costs.
\item[\textbf{G3}] \emph{Two scales under one name.} Systems of this kind routinely publish
a continuous rubric score and a binary defect indicator under a single metric, and average
them into one cell. A Gaussian mean over a mixture of a $0$--$100$ rubric and a two-point
Bernoulli is not a quantity. Section~\ref{sec:reward} separates them.
\item[\textbf{G4}] \emph{Leakage in the covariates.} Where a size class is computed from
the artefact's token count \emph{after} the run, it cannot be conditioned on before the
pick. A feature known only after the outcome is not a feature.
\end{enumerate}
A rule with no units, moreover, cannot be told what a failure costs, which is the
motivation for everything in Section~\ref{sec:decision}.

\section{The reward: an executable grade, and what a proxy is worth}
\label{sec:reward}

\begin{definition}[The grade]
\label{def:grade}
For an artefact $\ell$, the harness runs a fixed, executable battery --- in the software
setting: a build over the whole module; a static check; the test suites of exactly the
packages the change touched, with the cache defeated; and a formatting check. Define
$g(\ell)=1$ if all succeed and $0$ otherwise. The grade is a function of the artefact and
the repository alone. No language model is consulted, so no model grades itself, and no
model's opinion of an artefact can move it.
\end{definition}

This is a deliberately narrow reward. It says nothing about whether the change is the
change that was asked for; Section~\ref{sec:limits} treats that as the principal threat to
validity. What it buys is a reward that is cheap, reproducible by a stranger, and immune
to the single largest hazard in an agent harness --- that the thing being optimised is a
claim made by the thing being optimised.

\subsection{Auxiliary observations and their reliability}

Other signals exist and are not thrown away: an independent rating in $[0,100]$; the
reviewing role's counts of major and minor findings; the run's own account of how it
stopped. They enter as observations \emph{of} $g$ with a fitted reliability, never as $g$.

\begin{proposition}[What one noisy binary observation is worth]
\label{prop:reliab}
Let $g\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ and let $z\in\{0,1\}$ satisfy $P(z{=}1\mid g{=}1)=a$ and
$P(z{=}0\mid g{=}0)=b$ with $\gamma:=a+b-1>0$, and $z\perp\!\!\!\perp$ everything else
given $g$. Then $z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)$ with $q=ap+(1-b)(1-p)$, and the Fisher
information $z$ carries about $p$ is $\gamma^2/\big(q(1-q)\big)$. Relative to one direct
observation of $g$, which carries $1/\big(p(1-p)\big)$, the \emph{effective count} of one
$z$ is
\begin{equation}
\rho \;=\; \gamma^{2}\,\frac{p(1-p)}{q(1-q)}\ \in[0,1],
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
with $\rho=1$ iff $a=b=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$q=ap+(1-b)(1-p)$ gives $dq/dp=a-(1-b)=\gamma$. The Fisher information of a
Bernoulli$(q)$ observation about its own parameter is $1/(q(1-q))$; by the chain rule for
Fisher information under a smooth reparametrisation, its information about $p$ is
$(dq/dp)^2/(q(1-q))=\gamma^2/(q(1-q))$. Dividing by $1/(p(1-p))$ gives~\eqref{eq:rho}. For
the range, write $\gamma=a+b-1$ and note $q(1-q)\ge \gamma^2 p(1-p)$ because $q$ is an
affine image of $p$ with slope $\gamma$ into $[0,1]$:
$\operatorname{Var}(z)\ge\operatorname{Var}(\E[z\mid g])=\gamma^2 p(1-p)$, the inequality
being the law of total variance with the conditional variance dropped, and it is an
equality exactly when $\operatorname{Var}(z\mid g)=0$ almost surely, i.e.\ $a=b=1$.
\end{proof}

An observation from source $\mathrm{src}\in\srcs$ is therefore admitted with weight
$\rho_{\mathrm{src}}$ in the count $N$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend} and its successors, and
$(a_{\mathrm{src}},b_{\mathrm{src}})$ are fitted wherever grades and proxies co-occur --- a
Dawid--Skene fit over sources~\cite{dawid1979}, the binary case of the many-facet Rasch
calibration~\cite{linacre1989} that a rater-severity fit performs on the continuous scale.

\subsection{Three proxies, measured}

Figure~\ref{fig:selfreport} applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho} to a ledger of $202$ graded runs
(Section~\ref{sec:exp} describes it), using ``the run carried no major finding'' as the
grade, for want of an executable grader in the historical data. Three results, and the
third is a design consequence.

\emph{A run's own claim is worth almost nothing.} The harness's own verdict that a run
finished has sensitivity $0.56$, specificity $0.62$, $\gamma=0.18$ and $\rho=0.02$: fifty
such claims carry the information of one grade. The process exit code does barely better
($\gamma=0.25$, $\rho=0.04$). An independent rating of $4$ or $5$ on a five-point scale
reaches $\gamma=0.72$, $\rho=0.42$.

\emph{The relation between a rating and a defect is not monotone.} At ratings $2$ and $3$
the chance of at least one major finding is $1.00$ and $0.81$; at rating $1$ it is $0.12$.
The reason is visible in the run records: $92\%$ of rating-$1$ runs produced nothing at all
--- they hit a cap or a provider error with no artefact. A run that does nothing has
nothing to be wrong about. Any reward monotone in the rating would pay for doing nothing,
and any reward monotone in the \emph{absence} of findings would pay for it too.
Definition~\ref{def:grade} does not, because an empty artefact fails the tests the brief
asked for.

\emph{Therefore the scoreboard splits.} There is one Bernoulli metric, acceptance, whose
cells carry a source dimension in $\srcs$ and whose per-source $(a,b)$ are fitted
alongside any rater severities; and a separate continuous metric on its own scale. The two
are never summed into one cell. This is the resolution of G3.

\section{The decision: one dial in dollars}
\label{sec:decision}

\begin{definition}[The objective]
For a task with context $x$ and a stake $L\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
J(a;x,L)\;=\;\E\big[B(a,x)\big]\;+\;L\,\Big(1-P\big(g{=}1\mid a,x\big)\Big),
\label{eq:J}
\end{equation}
and the pick is $a^\star(x,L)=\argmin_{a\in\acts}J(a;x,L)$.
\end{definition}

$L$ is the whole user-facing interface: the number of dollars a task that has to be redone,
or that ships a defect, costs this practitioner. Latency enters, when it is wanted, as
$J+\lambda\,\E[t(a,x)]$ with $\lambda$ dollars per minute; every statement below survives
that addition unchanged, since it only adds a second deterministic term to the bill.

Write $b(a)=\E[B(a,x)]$ and $f(a)=1-P(g{=}1\mid a,x)$ at a fixed $x$, and let
$P=\{(b(a),f(a)):a\in\acts\}\subset\R_{\ge0}\times[0,1]$ be the achievable set.

\begin{proposition}[What the dial can reach]
\label{prop:hull}
$a$ minimises $J(\cdot;x,L)$ for some $L\in[0,\infty)$ if and only if $(b(a),f(a))$ is an
exposed point of $\conv(P)+\R^2_{\ge0}$ in a direction $(1,L)$ --- equivalently, a vertex
of the lower-left convex hull of $P$. Every such $a$ is Pareto-optimal; the converse fails.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$J(a;x,L)=\langle(1,L),(b(a),f(a))\rangle$ is a linear functional with strictly positive
first and non-negative second coefficient. A point of a finite set minimises a linear
functional with non-negative coefficients over the set iff it minimises it over the convex
hull, and the minimisers of such a functional over a polytope are exactly the faces exposed
by that direction; ranging $L$ over $[0,\infty)$ ranges the direction over all of
$\{(1,L):L\ge0\}$, whose exposed points are the vertices of the lower-left hull. That a
minimiser is Pareto-optimal for $L>0$ follows because a dominating point would attain a
strictly smaller value. The converse fails already for three points: with
$P=\{(0.1,0.40),(0.5,0.30),(1.0,0.10)\}$ the middle point is Pareto-optimal but lies
strictly above the segment joining the other two (which passes through
$(0.5,0.2\overline{6})$), so no $(1,L)$ exposes it. This is the standard phenomenon of
\emph{unsupported} efficient solutions in bi-objective combinatorial
optimisation~\cite{geoffrion1968,ehrgott2005}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The sweep is a finite monotone path]
\label{prop:sweep}
Order the lower-hull vertices by increasing bill, $a_0,\dots,a_K$. Then
$L\mapsto b(a^\star(L))$ is non-decreasing and $L\mapsto f(a^\star(L))$ is non-increasing;
$a^\star(L)=a_j$ exactly for $L$ in the interval $[L_j,L_{j+1}]$ with
\begin{equation}
L_j=\frac{b(a_j)-b(a_{j-1})}{f(a_{j-1})-f(a_j)},\qquad L_0=0,\ L_{K+1}=\infty,
\label{eq:breakpoints}
\end{equation}
and $L_1\le L_2\le\dots\le L_K$. Each $L_j$ is the dollars per unit of failure probability
that the $j$th upgrade buys.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consecutive hull vertices $a_{j-1},a_j$ are indifferent exactly when
$b(a_{j-1})+Lf(a_{j-1})=b(a_j)+Lf(a_j)$, which rearranges to~\eqref{eq:breakpoints}; the
hull's edge slopes $-1/L_j$ increase along the hull by convexity, so the $L_j$ increase,
and the argmin steps from $a_{j-1}$ to $a_j$ at $L_j$ and nowhere else. Monotonicity of
$b$ and $f$ along the sweep is then monotonicity along the hull, which holds by
construction.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sweep} is what makes the dial implementable: the hull is computed
once, in $O(|\acts|\log|\acts|)$ after the front, and the answer for any $L$ is a binary
search over at most $K+1$ intervals. It is also what makes the dial explainable: $L_j$ is a
sentence --- ``spending $\$0.90$ more per task buys one point of acceptance''.

\subsection{Retiring the knee, and why the usual argument for retiring it is wrong}

One expects to show that the knee of Eq.~\eqref{eq:knee} is unreachable. It is not.

\begin{proposition}[The knee is always supported]
\label{prop:kneehull}
Let the front have at least two distinct qualities, so the chord's slope
$g=(Q_1-Q_0)/(\ln B_1-\ln B_0)$ is strictly positive, and let $a_p$ maximise the bracket
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:knee} over the front. Then $(b(a_p),f(a_p))$ is a vertex of the lower-left
convex hull of $P$. Consequently the knee is the argmin of $J(\cdot;L)$ for a non-empty
interval of $L$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $D(a)=Q(a)-Q_0-g(\ln B(a)-\ln B_0)$, so $D(a_p)\ge D(a)$ for all $a$ on the front,
and recall $Q=100(1-f)$ so that ``above the chord in $Q$'' and ``below the chord in $f$''
are the same statement. Suppose $a_p$ is not a hull vertex. Then there are front points
$a_u,a_v$ with $B_u<B_p<B_v$ such that $a_p$ lies strictly above the segment
$\overline{a_ua_v}$ in $(B,f)$, equivalently strictly below it in $(B,Q)$:
\[
Q_p \;<\; Q_u+t\,(Q_v-Q_u),\qquad t=\frac{B_p-B_u}{B_v-B_u}\in(0,1),
\]
i.e.\ $(1-t)(Q_p-Q_u)<t\,(Q_v-Q_p)$. Optimality of $a_p$ gives $D(a_p)\ge D(a_u)$ and
$D(a_p)\ge D(a_v)$, that is
\[
Q_p-Q_u\ \ge\ g\ln(B_p/B_u),\qquad Q_v-Q_p\ \le\ g\ln(B_v/B_p).
\]
Combining, $(1-t)\,g\ln(B_p/B_u)\le(1-t)(Q_p-Q_u)<t\,(Q_v-Q_p)\le t\,g\ln(B_v/B_p)$, and
since $g>0$,
\begin{equation}
(1-t)\ln\!\big(B_p/B_u\big)\;<\;t\,\ln\!\big(B_v/B_p\big).
\label{eq:lnineq}
\end{equation}
But $B_p=(1-t)B_u+tB_v$, so by concavity of $\ln$,
$\ln B_p\ge(1-t)\ln B_u+t\ln B_v$, which rearranges to exactly
$(1-t)\ln(B_p/B_u)\ge t\ln(B_v/B_p)$, contradicting~\eqref{eq:lnineq}. Hence $a_p$ is a
hull vertex, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:hull} some $L\ge0$ selects it.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The proof uses only that the knee's scalarisation is affine in a \emph{concave} increasing
transform of the bill. Any such transform --- $\ln$, $\sqrt{\ }$, a Box--Cox with
$\lambda\le1$ --- gives the same conclusion. A \emph{convex} transform would not, and a
knee measured on a convex transform of the bill can indeed be unsupported.
\end{remark}

So the knee is a decision. The objection is a different one, and it is fatal.

\begin{proposition}[The knee's stake is covariant with the price scale; the practitioner's is not]
\label{prop:scale}
Multiply every price in the catalog by $\sigma>0$, so $b\mapsto\sigma b$ and $f$ is
unchanged. Then (i) the knee of Eq.~\eqref{eq:knee} is unchanged; (ii)
$\argmin_a\{\sigma b(a)+Lf(a)\}=\argmin_a\{b(a)+(L/\sigma)f(a)\}$, so the assignment that a
fixed stake $L$ selects is the assignment that the stake $L/\sigma$ selected before. If the
lower hull has at least two vertices, there is an open interval of $\sigma$ on which the
two disagree.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) $\ln(\sigma B)-\ln(\sigma B_0)=\ln B-\ln B_0$, and likewise for the denominator, so the
bracket of~\eqref{eq:knee} is unchanged. (ii) $\sigma b+Lf=\sigma\big(b+(L/\sigma)f\big)$
and multiplication by $\sigma>0$ preserves the argmin. By Proposition~\ref{prop:sweep},
$L\mapsto a^\star(L)$ is a non-constant step function whenever $K\ge1$, so
$a^\star(L/\sigma)\ne a^\star(L)$ for $\sigma$ in an interval around any breakpoint ratio.
\end{proof}

This is the whole case. Prices in this market move by factors of two in months. Under a
uniform halving of prices the correct decision moves \emph{up} the front, because a fixed
number of dollars of stake now buys twice as much quality; the knee does not move at all,
because scale-invariance is what it was designed for. The right panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:hull} draws that: the knee is a horizontal line, and the $L=\$5$ pick is a
staircase crossing it. Worse, the stake the knee \emph{implies} is never shown: on the
simulated front of Figure~\ref{fig:hull} the knee sits in the interval
$L\in[\$3.57,\$18.46]$, a range in which the practitioner was never asked to have an
opinion.

\section{Price bands}
\label{sec:bands}

\begin{definition}[Band]
Fix a base $\beta>1$ and a reference price $\pi_{\min}$. Model $m$'s band at role $r$ is
$\mathrm{band}_r(m)=\big\lfloor \log_\beta\big(c_{r,m}/\pi_{\min}\big)\big\rfloor$, where
$c_{r,m}$ is the role's expected bill for one task under the token shape of
Section~\ref{sec:cost}. We use $\beta=2$: a band is a doubling of the bill.
\end{definition}

Bands do two jobs and neither of them is the decision.

\emph{Pruning.} $|\acts|$ is a product over roles. With hundreds of priced models the full
assignment set is far too large to score, and a quality floor --- the usual device --- can
delete a whole price region. A band-aware shortlist keeps at most $k$ models per band per
role, the $k$ with the largest posterior mean of $\theta_{r,m}$, which bounds $|\acts|$ at
$(k\cdot\#\text{bands})^{|\roles|}$ and cannot delete a price region. We use $k=3$. The
rule is a heuristic and we do not claim it preserves the hull; an assignment whose only
virtue is that it is the fourth-best in its band is lost.

\emph{Pooling exploration.} A model with no observations borrows, through the hierarchy of
Section~\ref{sec:quality}, from its band:
$\theta_{r,m}\sim\mathcal N(w_r^\top\varphi_m+\mu_{r,\mathrm{band}(m)},\,\sigma_0^2)$ with a
band offset $\mu_{r,b}$ itself drawn from $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_b^2)$. Because bills within
a band differ by less than a factor of $\beta$, the exploration budget of
Section~\ref{sec:decide} can be spent on any member of a band at a known worst-case cost,
which is what makes a hard dollar cap enforceable in advance rather than in arrears.

\emph{What bands are not.} They are not the decision. A band is a bin over one coordinate
of $P$; the decision is Proposition~\ref{prop:hull}. Reporting a band instead of a stake
would reintroduce exactly the unit-free knob just retired.

\section{Quality: per-role decomposition, prior, sample cost, drift}
\label{sec:quality}

\begin{assumption}[Additive logit]
\label{ass:add}
\begin{equation}
\logit P\big(g{=}1\mid a,x\big)\;=\;\alpha_x+\sum_{r\in\roles}\theta_{r,a(r)} .
\label{eq:logit}
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{What it buys.}
The unknowns are $\sum_r|\mods_r|$ numbers rather than $|\acts|$. Feedback is
\emph{semi-bandit}: the grade of one run is one observation for every role on that
assignment, so a run informs every assignment that shares a model with it. This is the
structure of combinatorial bandits with semi-bandit feedback~\cite{chen2013,wen2015}, and
it is the difference between learning tens of numbers and learning tens of thousands.

\paragraph{What it ignores.}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:logit} has no interaction. The interaction one most expects is real and has
a name: a strong reviewer rescuing a weak writer, so that $P(g{=}1)$ for (cheap writer,
dear reviewer) exceeds the additive prediction. We do not fit it as a parameter. We capture
the largest part of it structurally, by the cascade of Section~\ref{sec:cascade}, which is
an explicit two-stage action whose value is computed rather than learned. A pair term
$\delta_{(m_r,m_{r'})}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_{rr'}^2)$ can be added as a further level of
the hierarchy below and costs observations only where a pair is actually run. We flag any
claim about its size as conjecture --- and Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports a hindsight result
that the additive model gets wrong for exactly this reason, which is the strongest evidence
we have that the term is worth fitting.

\subsection{The prior, and the three-source posterior}

\begin{assumption}[Hierarchy]
\label{ass:hier}
With $\varphi_m$ the catalog indexes and $w_r$ the role's weighting,
\begin{equation}
\theta_{r,m}\sim\mathcal N\big(w_r^\top\varphi_m+\mu_{r,\mathrm{band}(m)},\ \sigma_0^2\big),\qquad
\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}\mid\theta_{r,m}\sim\mathcal N\big(\theta_{r,m},\ \sigma_u^2\big),
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
where $\theta^{(i)}$ is site $i$'s own value and $\sigma_u^2$ is the between-site variance.
Observations carry variance $\sigma^2$.
\end{assumption}

Every priced model has a prior mean on the day it appears, which is the cold-start property
the catalog is for.

\begin{proposition}[Three-source posterior]
\label{prop:post}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:hier}, with $k=\sigma^2/\sigma_0^2$, $\ell=\sigma^2/\sigma_u^2$,
$\tilde n=n^{\mathrm{pool}}\ell/(\ell+1)$ and $K=k+\tilde n$, the posterior of site $i$'s
$\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}$ given the catalog, the pool and $n^i$ own observations is normal with
\begin{equation}
\E\big[\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}\mid\cdot\big]=\frac{k^{\ast}\,\hat\theta^{\mathrm{cat}} + n^{\ast}\,\bar y^{\mathrm{pool}} + n^{i}\,\bar y^{i}}{k^{\ast}+n^{\ast}+n^{i}},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}\big[\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}\mid\cdot\big]=\frac{\sigma^2}{k^{\ast}+n^{\ast}+n^{i}},
\label{eq:three}
\end{equation}
where everything external to this site shares one capped count
\begin{equation}
W=\frac{K\,\ell}{K+\ell}\ \le\ \ell,\qquad k^{\ast}=\frac{k}{K}W,\qquad n^{\ast}=\frac{\tilde n}{K}W .
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Integrate out $\theta_{r,m}$. The catalog and the pool inform $\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}$ only
through $\theta_{r,m}$, whose posterior given them is normal with precision $K/\sigma^2$
and mean the $k:\tilde n$ precision-weighted average of $\hat\theta^{\mathrm{cat}}$ and
$\bar y^{\mathrm{pool}}$; each pool observation is another site's, hence carries variance
$\sigma^2+\sigma_u^2$ about $\theta_{r,m}$, which is the deflation
$\tilde n=n^{\mathrm{pool}}\ell/(\ell+1)$. Adding the between-site variance $\sigma_u^2$
gives, for $\theta^{(i)}$, precision $1/(\sigma^2/K+\sigma_u^2)=W/\sigma^2$ with the same
mean. Own observations add precision $n^i/\sigma^2$ directly. Precision-weighting the two
gives~\eqref{eq:three}, and $k^\ast+n^\ast=W$ split in the ratio $k:\tilde n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:blend}
With $\sigma_u=0$ ($\ell=\infty$) and no band offset, Eq.~\eqref{eq:three} is the plain
shrinkage blend $(1-w)\hat\theta^{\mathrm{cat}}+w\bar y$ with $w=n/(n+k)$,
$n=n^{\mathrm{pool}}+n^i$: exactly Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend}, with $\kappa=k=30$. Baseline B1 is
therefore the degenerate case of Proposition~\ref{prop:post} in which every other site's
run is assumed to be as informative as one's own.
\end{corollary}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:cap} is the point of the hierarchy: however many rows the pool holds,
everything external to this site is worth at most $\ell=\sigma^2/\sigma_u^2$ of this site's
own observations, because other sites' tasks are not this site's tasks; own observations
are never capped. Section~\ref{sec:federation} gives $\ell$ its estimator.

\subsection{How many runs it costs to tell two models apart}

\begin{proposition}[Sample complexity at one role]
\label{prop:samples}
Let two models at role $r$ differ by $\Delta$ in $P(g{=}1)$ at a common operating point
$\bar p$, let each have $\kappa$ prior observations and $N$ graded ones, and approximate the
grade's variance by $\bar p(1-\bar p)$. Under the conjugate normal approximation the
posterior means differ by a normal with variance $2\bar p(1-\bar p)/(\kappa+N)$, so a
two-sided test at level $z$ separates them when
\begin{equation}
N\ \ge\ \frac{2z^{2}\,\bar p(1-\bar p)}{\Delta^{2}}\ -\ \kappa .
\label{eq:samples}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from the conjugate posterior $\mathcal N(\cdot,\sigma^2/(\kappa+N))$ with
$\sigma^2=\bar p(1-\bar p)$, independence of the two arms, and the definition of a
$z$-level two-sided test.
\end{proof}

At $z=1.96$ and $\bar p=0.75$ (so $\bar p(1-\bar p)=0.1875$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:samples} gives,
per model, at one role:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}\toprule
$\Delta$ & $\kappa=0$ & $\kappa=10$ & $\kappa=30$ & $\kappa=100$\\\midrule
$0.10$ & $144$ & $134$ & $114$ & $44$\\
$0.20$ & $36$ & $26$ & $6$ & $0$\\
$0.30$ & $16$ & $6$ & $0$ & $0$\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}[The subtraction is a loan, not a gift]
\label{rem:loan}
The $-\kappa$ in~\eqref{eq:samples} is only a saving if the prior is centred correctly. A
prior worth $\kappa$ observations that is wrong by more than $\Delta$ does not shorten the
experiment; it lengthens it, because the first $\kappa$ observations are spent undoing the
prior. Baseline B1's $\kappa=30$ is a constant, not an estimate;
Section~\ref{sec:federation} gives the moment estimator that should replace it, and
Section~\ref{sec:decide} shows what an overconfident $\kappa$ costs in the one regime where
it matters most.
\end{remark}

Two corollaries. First, $\Delta=0.10$ is out of reach for a single site: $114$ graded runs
\emph{per model per role}, against $202$ runs in total across all models in the ledger of
Section~\ref{sec:exp}. Pooling is not an optimisation; it is the only way the question is
answerable at all. Second, semi-bandit feedback is what makes even $\Delta=0.20$ plausible,
because each run pays for $|\roles|$ of these counters at once.

\subsection{Drift}

Models change under one identifier. Between observations, inflate the variance:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{r,m,t}=\theta_{r,m,t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N\big(0,\tau^2\Delta t\big),
\qquad V_t = \min\big(\sigma_0^2,\ V_{t-1}+\tau^2\Delta t\big),
\label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
which is the state equation of a local-level model~\cite{harvey1989} and the
variance-inflation step of Glicko-2~\cite{glickman2001}; the cap at the prior variance is
what keeps an arm from becoming \emph{more} uncertain than a model nobody has ever run. Two
consequences. An arm nobody has run for a while regains uncertainty and is drawn again by
the sampler of Section~\ref{sec:decide} --- forgetting is what makes exploration
self-renewing without an exploration rate. And a model identifier that is re-released is a
change point: the mean and the variance both reset to the prior, rather than drifting
there. Figure~\ref{fig:drift} draws both. $\tau^2$ is estimated by the day-to-day moment
$\hat\tau^2=[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\bar y_d-\bar y_{d-1})-\sigma^2(1/n_d+1/n_{d-1})]_+$
pooled over cells, which is why the day is a cell dimension; a cell with no evidence of
drift gets $\hat\tau^2=0$ and forgets nothing. The non-stationary bandit
literature~\cite{garivier2011} offers discounted and sliding-window alternatives; we prefer
variance inflation because it leaves the posterior mean alone and therefore does not throw
away information that has not been contradicted.

\centering\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/f7-drift.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Simulated.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:drift}. An arm run daily for three weeks, then
never; its posterior standard deviation climbs back toward the prior at a rate set by
$\tau$, which is what makes it eligible to be drawn again. At day $60$ the identifier is
re-released and the arm is reset rather than drifted.}
\label{fig:drift}
\end{figure}

\section{Cost: a learned token shape}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{definition}[Token shape]
$T_{r,m}=(\bar\iota_{r,m},\bar\gamma_{r,m},\bar o_{r,m},\bar\nu_{r,m})$: mean input tokens
per call, the fraction of them served from cache, mean output tokens per call, and mean
calls per task, for model $m$ in role $r$.
\end{definition}

The bill is then
\begin{equation}
\E[B(a,x)]=\sum_{r\in\roles}\bar\nu_{r,a(r)}(x)\Big[(1-\bar\gamma)\,\bar\iota\,\pi_{a(r)}+\bar\gamma\,\bar\iota\,\pi^c_{a(r)}+\bar o\,\chi_{a(r)}\Big] ,
\label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}
with the four shape components read at the task's context $x$. Each is estimated from the
site's own usage ledger with a shrinkage prior at the role's default shape:
\begin{equation}
\hat T_{r,m}=\frac{n_{r,m}\,\bar T_{r,m}+\kappa_T\,T^{\mathrm{def}}_{r}}{n_{r,m}+\kappa_T},
\label{eq:shape}
\end{equation}
a model with no history priced exactly as the baseline prices it, a model with history
priced as it actually behaves.

\paragraph{The shrinkage is on the log scale, and this is not a detail.}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shape} is written as an arithmetic average, which is what a conjugate normal
model prescribes for a quantity whose errors are additive. The volume share of a role is
not such a quantity: it is bounded in $[0,1]$, heavy-tailed, and enters the bill
multiplicatively. Section~\ref{sec:exp} measures the difference and it is the entire
effect: shrinking the arithmetic mean of the role's volume share improves leave-one-out
bill prediction from a mean absolute percentage error of $302\%$ to $242\%$, while
shrinking its logarithm --- that is, replacing the arithmetic mean in~\eqref{eq:shape} by a
geometric one for the volume components --- improves it to $58\%$. The reason is visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:cost}: a reviewing role takes a few per cent of a run's tokens on most runs
and most of them on a few, so the arithmetic mean of its share is not the share of any
typical run, and a rule that prices the typical run from that mean is wrong on nearly
every run.

\paragraph{The expectation is over task volumes, not at the median task.}
$J$ takes an expectation, so the cost model must integrate~\eqref{eq:bill} over the
distribution of task volumes rather than evaluate it at a representative task. The
distribution is heavy-tailed and its mean is not its median;
Section~\ref{sec:exp} shows that pricing the median task understates the mean bill of an
assignment by a factor that varies across assignments, which reorders them.

\section{Exploration}
\label{sec:decide}

The pick under Eq.~\eqref{eq:J} at posterior means never tries an assignment the prior
underrates. Thompson sampling~\cite{thompson1933,russo2018} draws one plausible world and
acts as if it were true.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{One pick}
\label{alg:pick}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require task context $x$; stake $L$; per-role shortlists $M_r$ (at most $k$ per band, Section~\ref{sec:bands}); the day's remaining exploration budget $E_{\mathrm{left}}$; the incumbent assignment $a_0$
\For{each role $r$ and $m\in M_r$}
  \State $\tilde\theta_{r,m}\sim\mathcal N(\mu_{r,m},V_{r,m})$ \Comment{one draw per task; never redrawn inside a task}
\EndFor
\State $\tilde a \gets \argmin_{a}\ \E[B(a,x)] + L\big(1-\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\alpha_x+\sum_r\tilde\theta_{r,a(r)})\big)$ \Comment{costs are \emph{not} sampled}
\State $\hat a \gets$ the same argmin at the posterior means $\mu$ \Comment{the greedy assignment}
\State $e \gets \big[\E[B(\tilde a,x)]-\E[B(\hat a,x)]\big]_+$ \Comment{what this draw would spend on exploration}
\If{$e > E_{\mathrm{left}}$} \State $\tilde a\gets\hat a$;\ $e\gets0$ \Comment{the hard daily cap}
\EndIf
\State $\tilde a \gets$ the assignment in $\{a_0\}\cup\{a:\ a\ \text{differs from}\ a_0\ \text{in at most one role}\}$ nearest $\tilde a$ in $J$ \Comment{one role moves per task}
\State \Return $\tilde a$ if $J(a_0)-J(\tilde a)>\delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:switch}), else $a_0$;\quad $E_{\mathrm{left}}\mathrel{-}=e$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Four things in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pick} deserve their own justification.

\emph{The draw is inside $J$, not beside it.} Because costs are deterministic and $L$ is in
dollars, an expensive assignment is drawn only when the sampled quality gain covers its
extra bill at the practitioner's own stake. There is no separate exploration rate to set
and no unit-free temperature.

\emph{A hard daily cap $E$.} Thompson sampling has no budget; $J$ prices exploration but
does not bound it. $E$ is a per-site, per-day cap on
$\sum_t[B(\tilde a_t)-B(\hat a_t)]_+$, which makes the worst case a number the
practitioner can be told. This is the bandits-with-knapsacks setting~\cite{bwk2013} in its
simplest form --- one resource, a hard constraint --- and we do not use its algorithms; we
use the constraint, and we say plainly that greedily spending the budget until it is
exhausted is not the optimal way to spend it.

\emph{One role changes per task.} Changing two roles at once makes the resulting grade
uninformative about which change caused it, and makes bills jump. The restriction costs
optimality --- the reachable set in one step is a neighbourhood, not $\acts$ --- and buys
attribution and stability.

\emph{Cheap exploration has its own vehicle.} A one-token probe action buys a measurement
at negligible cost, and reserving a slot for the least-observed pair is the same idea at
the task level. Observations from a deliberately exploratory slot carry a source tag and
hence, by Proposition~\ref{prop:reliab}, their own reliability; they are not the same
evidence as a run the rule chose on its merits.

\subsection{What the simulation says, including the part we did not want}

Figure~\ref{fig:regret} is a calibrated simulation: two roles, ten models each, bills
log-uniform over the two orders of magnitude the measured ledger spans, quality additive on
the probability scale, grades Bernoulli, estimation the exact Gaussian linear model (so one
grade updates both roles --- the semi-bandit structure), prior the truth plus noise held at
strength $\kappa$.

\emph{Where every model carries a catalog index, exploration does not repay itself.} Over
$800$ tasks the posterior-mean rule accumulates \$195 of regret, Thompson inside $J$
accumulates \$347, and the capped version \$270. Thompson's instantaneous regret falls
below the greedy rule's only at around $T\approx2000$ in a longer run, and the cumulative
debt is not repaid within any horizon a single site will see. The reason is not subtle: a
Bernoulli grade has standard deviation near $0.45$ about a quantity whose spread across
models is near $0.15$, so evidence arrives slowly, while $L=\$5$ makes a quality
uncertainty of $0.04$ worth $\$0.20$ --- more than the whole bill of a cheap assignment. We
checked the sensitivity: at $L\in\{\$1,\$5,\$20,\$80\}$ the ratio of Thompson's regret to
the greedy rule's is $1.36$, $1.51$, $1.70$, $1.73$. The finding is robust in the direction
we did not want.

\emph{Where one model per role carries no index, the greedy rule fails completely.} Give
each role one model the catalog does not index --- a new release, an open-weights
checkpoint, anything outside the feed --- give it a prior equal to the role's average with
three times the prior variance, and make it in fact the best model at its role. The
posterior-mean rule never plays it: its regret over $800$ tasks is \$839 and its per-task
regret in the last hundred tasks is \$1.00, not falling. Thompson finds it: \$301
cumulative, \$0.19 per task and still falling. The cap costs almost nothing here (\$273
cumulative regret, \$4 of exploration spend against \$70 uncapped) because the exploration
that matters is cheap and early.

\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f6-regret.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Simulated}, calibrated to the measured ledger's cost spread. Left: with
every model indexed, the posterior-mean rule beats Thompson sampling inside $J$ over any
horizon a site will see. Middle: with one unindexed model per role that happens to be the

\paragraph{The design conclusion.}
Exploration is not worth paying for in the assignment choice at this data scale when the
prior covers every arm; it is worth almost any price when the prior does not cover an arm.
So: keep the posterior-mean pick as the default, keep the Thompson draw behind a setting,
keep the hard cap $E$ small, and direct the budget at arms whose \emph{prior} is
uninformative rather than at arms whose posterior is merely wide. That is a
value-of-information argument, and it is the argument for IDS~\cite{russo2014ids,russo2018ids}
or a knowledge-gradient-per-dollar rule over plain Thompson sampling. We do not implement
either, for a stated reason: both need a per-candidate information estimate at every pick,
and at $|\acts|$ in the thousands and $|\mods|$ in the hundreds that is a second
optimisation inside the first, while the gain over Thompson sampling at $T$ in the hundreds
is within the noise of Figure~\ref{fig:regret}. We regard ``IDS beats Thompson-in-$J$ on
this problem'' as a conjecture and have not tested it.

\subsection{Regret, as a sketch}

\begin{proposition}[Sketch]
\label{prop:regret}
Suppose (i) $P(g{=}1\mid a,x)$ is replaced by its linearisation $\nu^\top\theta_a$ about the
operating point, so that $J(a;L)=b(a)+L(1-\nu^\top\theta_a)$ is affine in $\theta$ with a
known deterministic offset $b(a)$; (ii) the prior is the true generating prior; (iii) grades
are conditionally sub-Gaussian with proxy $\varsigma$. Then the Bayesian regret of Thompson
sampling over $T$ tasks satisfies $\mathcal R(T)=O\big(L\,\varsigma\sqrt{d\,T\log T}\big)$
with $d=\sum_r|M_r|$, by the general posterior-sampling bound of Russo and Van
Roy~\cite{russo2014}, and the $d$ rather than $|\acts|$ is exactly the semi-bandit structure
exploited by the combinatorial analyses of Chen, Wang and Yuan~\cite{chen2013} and Wen,
Kveton and Ashkan~\cite{wen2015}; for combinatorial Thompson sampling specifically see Wang
and Chen~\cite{wangchen2018}.
\end{proposition}

We label this a sketch and mean it. Assumption (i) is false --- the link is a logit and the
linearisation error is not controlled; assumption (ii) is false, and
Section~\ref{sec:decide} showed the failure of (ii) is what actually governs the outcome;
the band pruning of Section~\ref{sec:bands} changes $\acts$ between rounds, which none of
these analyses covers; and the exploration cap makes the policy non-Thompson on the rounds
where it binds. The bound is offered as an account of \emph{why} the dimension is $d$ and
not $|\acts|$, and for nothing else. The empirical statement in Figure~\ref{fig:regret} is
not an instance of it.

\section{Cascades}
\label{sec:cascade}

Extend the action set. A \emph{cascade} $(b\to b')$ runs an assignment from band $b$; if
the grade is $0$, it escalates to an assignment from band $b'>b$; if that also fails, the
stake is paid.

\begin{proposition}[Break-even]
\label{prop:cascade}
Write $c_b,c_{b'}$ for the two bands' expected bills and $p_b,p_{b'}$ for their acceptance
probabilities, and let $X=c_{b'}+(1-p_{b'})L$ be the cost of going straight to $b'$. Then
\begin{align}
J(b\to b') &= c_b+(1-p_b)\big[c_{b'}+(1-p_{b'})L\big],\label{eq:cascade}\\
J(b\to b') < J(b') &\iff p_b > \frac{c_b}{c_{b'}+(1-p_{b'})L}, \label{eq:cascadeA}\\
J(b\to b') < J(b)\ \ \ &\iff p_{b'}L > c_{b'} .\label{eq:cascadeB}
\end{align}
At $L=0$, \eqref{eq:cascadeA} is the bare cost ratio $p_b>c_b/c_{b'}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\eqref{eq:cascade} is the expectation over the first grade. Then
$J(b\to b')-J(b')=c_b+(1-p_b)X-X=c_b-p_bX$, giving~\eqref{eq:cascadeA}. And
$J(b\to b')-J(b)=(1-p_b)[c_{b'}+(1-p_{b'})L]-(1-p_b)L=(1-p_b)[c_{b'}-p_{b'}L]$, which is
negative iff $p_{b'}L>c_{b'}$, giving~\eqref{eq:cascadeB}. With a latency price $\lambda$
the same algebra holds with $c$ replaced by $c+\lambda t$ throughout.
\end{proof}

Read together the two conditions say: cascade when the cheap band clears the bar often
enough to pay for its own attempt, \emph{and} when the escalation is worth buying at the
practitioner's stake at all. Higher stakes lower the first bar and raise the second, because
a cascade ends unaccepted with probability $(1-p_b)(1-p_{b'})$, strictly less than
$(1-p_{b'})$: escalation buys quality as well as costing money. Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}
draws both. This is the mechanism FrugalGPT studies for single-call
inference~\cite{frugalgpt2023}; the difference here is that the escalation decision is made
on a model-free grade rather than on a scored confidence, which is what the next paragraph
is about.

\paragraph{The endogeneity hazard, and why an executable grade removes it.}
Suppose the escalation trigger were the reviewing role's verdict rather than the grade, and
suppose that role is part of the assignment being scored. Then a cheap reviewer that passes
bad work produces no escalation, the cheap writer's measured acceptance rises, the rule
prefers the cheap assignment, and the cheap reviewer is chosen again. The feedback loop is
closed and it collapses to the cheapest possible assignment with the worst possible quality,
while every measured quantity in the system improves. Formally, the trigger $z$ and the
outcome $g$ are both functions of the action $a$ through the reviewing role, so
$\E[z\mid a]$ is not an unbiased signal of $\E[g\mid a]$ and the argmin of the estimated $J$
is not consistent for the argmin of $J$. Definition~\ref{def:grade} breaks the loop by
construction: $g$ is computed from the artefact by the harness, and no model in the
assignment can move it. An independent rating and the reviewing role's findings remain as
auxiliary observations with reliability $\rho<1$, which is exactly the right treatment for a
signal that is informative and endogenous: it can shorten the experiment, and it cannot
determine its answer.

\section{Switching and stability}
\label{sec:switch}

\begin{definition}[Switching threshold]
Let $a_0$ be the incumbent and $a_1$ the challenger. Switch only at a task boundary, and
only when
\begin{equation}
J(a_0)-J(a_1) \;>\; \delta,\qquad \delta \;=\; \underbrace{\hat\iota^{\,\mathrm{cached}}\big(\pi_{a_0(r)}-\pi^c_{a_0(r)}\big)}_{\text{cache loss}}\;+\;h,
\label{eq:switch}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\iota^{\,\mathrm{cached}}$ is the prompt prefix currently cached for the role
being changed and $h\ge0$ is a hysteresis margin.
\end{definition}

The first term is a real cost with a receipt: changing the model in a role discards the
provider-side cache of that role's prompt prefix, and the next call pays the prompt price
for tokens that would have been served at the cache-read price. The second is a margin
against churn.

\begin{remark}[$\delta=0$ for a batch harness]
Where every task is a fresh session, there is no cached prefix to lose across tasks, so
$\hat\iota^{\,\mathrm{cached}}=0$ and $\delta=h$. Inside an interactive session the prefix
is real and $\delta>0$; this is why the model in a role is fixed for the duration of a task
and may change only between tasks.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Evaluating at every boundary inflates false switches.}
The rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:switch} is tested after every task, which is optional stopping: a
fixed-$\alpha$ test applied at every $t$ rejects with probability one under the null. The
correct instrument is an anytime-valid confidence sequence: a family $\{C_t\}$ with
$P(\exists t:\theta\notin C_t)\le\alpha$, built from a non-negative
supermartingale~\cite{howard2021}. Concretely, maintain the sequence for the difference
$J(a_0)-J(a_1)$ and switch when its lower bound exceeds $\delta$. The cost is a wider
interval than a fixed-sample test at the same $\alpha$; the benefit is that ``we checked at
every task'' is no longer a way of manufacturing a switch.

\paragraph{Why batching is the right shape.}
Bandits with switching costs have minimax regret $\tilde\Theta(T^{2/3})$ rather than
$\tilde\Theta(T^{1/2})$~\cite{dekel2014}, and the optimal policies batch: they hold an arm
for a stretch, then reconsider. The task boundary is a natural batch edge --- it is where
the cache is worthless anyway and where a bill is closed --- so the switching structure the
theory recommends is the structure the problem already has. We claim no bound; we claim that
the shape is not accidental.

\section{Pooling across sites}
\label{sec:federation}

\begin{definition}[Cell]
A cell is keyed by $(\text{role},\text{model},\text{source},\text{channel},\text{size},\text{day})$
and holds $(n,\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2)$: count, sum and sum of squares, plus the count of
contributing sites. A sheet maps (site, cell) to such a triple.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Merging.}
Two structures, deliberately different. \emph{Within} a store, a site's own sheet
accumulates its own disjoint observations by addition, which is associative and commutative
but not idempotent, so a retried batch can be double-counted and the relay bounds that with
a per-site daily quota. \emph{Between} stores, a copy of another store's sheet merges by
componentwise maximum on each site's running total, which is a join-semilattice and hence a
state-based CRDT~\cite{shapiro2011}: any two nodes that have seen the same publications, in
any order and with any duplication, hold the same sheet. The published index is a function
of the merged sheet, so a relay is a convenience for bandwidth and signing, not an
authority.

\paragraph{What this is and is not.}
It is centralised-relay and mergeable: one relay publishes, any node can host one, and two
relays that receive the same publications agree. It is \emph{not} decentralised learning.
There is no coordinated exploration --- nothing tells site $B$ that site $A$ has already
paid to measure a cell, so the pool re-measures what it already knows. Publishing a
per-cell value of information alongside each cell, so that sites allocate their exploration
budgets to disjoint questions, is the obvious next step and is exactly the
distributed-exploration problem~\cite{hillel2013}; federated bandit
formulations~\cite{shi2021} give the communication--regret trade-offs. We have implemented
none of it and claim none of it.

\paragraph{The site random effect gives $\ell$ its meaning.}
Assumption~\ref{ass:hier}'s $\theta^{(i)}_{r,m}=\theta_{r,m}+u_{i,r,m}$,
$u\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^2)$, is what makes $\ell=\sigma^2/\sigma_u^2$ the cap of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cap}: however large the pool, the outside world is worth at most $\ell$ of
this site's own runs. Every constant is a moment estimate on the sheet, none is set by hand:
\begin{align}
\hat\sigma^2 &= \frac{\sum_{i,\theta}\big(\Sigma_{2,i\theta}-\Sigma_{1,i\theta}^2/n_{i\theta}\big)}{\sum_{i,\theta}(n_{i\theta}-1)},
&&\hat\sigma_u^2 = \Big[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_i\big(\bar y_{i\theta}\big)-\hat\sigma^2\,\overline{(1/n_{i\theta})}\Big]_+,
\label{eq:moments}\\
\hat\sigma_0^2 &= \Big[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{(r,m)}\big(\bar y^{\mathrm{pool}}_{r,m}-\hat\theta^{\mathrm{cat}}_{r,m}\big)-\overline{(\hat\sigma^2+\hat\sigma_u^2)/n^{\mathrm{pool}}}\Big]_+,
&&\kappa=\hat\sigma^2/\hat\sigma_0^2,\quad \ell=\hat\sigma^2/\hat\sigma_u^2 ,
\nonumber
\end{align}
each a within-minus-noise estimator of the Efron--Morris kind~\cite{efron1975}. Each is
withheld until its denominator carries at least ten degrees of freedom, with the day-one
values of Table~\ref{tab:const} until then. On a small seed index $\hat\sigma_0^2$ clips to
zero --- a dozen cells of two-point scores cannot resolve a catalog error under about
thirteen points --- which is why $\kappa=30$ still stands as a day-one value and why
Remark~\ref{rem:loan} is not an academic caveat.

\paragraph{Robustness and reliability.}
Rows from one site share a task mix and a codebase; the unit of aggregation is therefore the
site mean, not the row. A Huber $M$-estimate over site means (MAD scale $1.4826$, tuning
constant $1.345$, breakdown $1/2$)~\cite{huber1964} bounds what a minority of broken or
adversarial sites can move, with a precision-weighted mean below five sites. Rater
severities are fitted as one additive effect per rater by alternating weighted means, which
is the two-way additive model identified up to one constant per connected component of the
rater-by-cell graph --- the many-facet Rasch calibration~\cite{linacre1989}. The same
machinery fits one $(a_{\mathrm{src}},b_{\mathrm{src}})$ per source~\cite{dawid1979} and
publishes $\rho_{\mathrm{src}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho} in the index, so that every site
discounts a proxy observation by the same factor.

Three quantities are fixed once here and used throughout. The \emph{failure bit} is
$1-g$ with $g$ taken to be the reviewing role's major-defect flag, negated, and with an
artefact that contains no change counted as a failure. This is a \emph{proxy} for
Definition~\ref{def:grade}, not the grade itself: the historical runs predate an executable
grader. Section~\ref{sec:reward} measured what that proxy is worth in the other direction
--- an independent rating carries $\rho=0.42$ of a grade --- and every claim below inherits
that qualification. The failure rate over the $136$ graded runs is $0.176$. The
\emph{assignment set} is the $12$ distinct assignments run at least three times, out of $15$
observed; the three dropped were run once each. The \emph{quality model} is the MAP of
Assumption~\ref{ass:add} under a catalog-anchored Gaussian prior worth $\kappa=30$ runs at
the operating point, with per-role anchors $\lambda(\varphi_m/\varphi_{\max}-\bar\varphi_r)$
at $\lambda=4$ logits; a model missing the primary index is imputed from the secondary one
by least squares over the $58$ catalog models carrying both ($r=0.88$).

\subsection{E1: what a role costs, and whether a learned shape predicts it}

Figure~\ref{fig:cost}, left, is the measured token shape. Over the $132$ runs carrying a
split, the writing role takes a median $0.974$ of a run's tokens (mean $0.877$), the
reviewing role a median $0.026$ (mean $0.117$) with a long tail --- its ninetieth
percentile is $0.395$ and its maximum $0.946$, and on nine of the $132$ runs the role that
only reads the work takes more than half the tokens --- and the planning role a median
below $10^{-3}$ (mean $0.006$). The dollars land somewhere else entirely: the writing
role takes a median $0.519$ of them and the reviewing role a median $0.437$. Restricted to
the $116$ runs in which the reviewing role was actually invoked, its token median is $0.034$
and its dollar median $0.509$. So the reviewing role's bill share is mostly price and partly
the volume tail. Against a fixed shape that assigns the reviewing role $0.08$ of a run's
tokens, the measured mean is $1.46$ times that ($1.67$ times on the invoked subset) while
the measured median is a third of it --- the fixed value is neither.

The prediction task is: given the assignment and the run's total token volume, return the
run's bill. Leave-one-out throughout; the shape for a run is fitted on the other $131$ runs
only, shrunk toward the fixed shape at $\kappa_T=30$; prices as published.

Two readings. First, the fixed shape is not merely noisy but biased: its median prediction
is $3.3$ times the realised bill, because it attributes $8\%$ of a large token volume to a
role whose price per token is often an order of magnitude higher than the writer's, and on
most runs that role does a small fraction of that work. Second, and this is the result we
did not expect, \emph{how} the shape is shrunk carries the entire effect. Shrinking the
arithmetic mean of the volume share recovers a fifth of the gap; shrinking its logarithm
recovers nine tenths of it. An ablation isolates the component: learning the volume share
alone gives MAPE $56\%$, while learning the cache share alone gives $299\%$ and the
input-to-output split alone $305\%$. The bill of a multi-role run is, to a first
approximation, a question about who spent the tokens.

At the assignment level --- predicting the mean bill of each of the $12$ assignments, which
is what $J$ needs --- the learned shape gives MAPE $38.9\%$ against $129.7\%$ for the fixed
one, and the fixed shape over-predicts by a median factor of $2.18$. That over-prediction is
not uniform across assignments, which is why it reorders them, and reordering is what E2
tests next.

\subsection{E2: does the rule pick what hindsight would have picked?}

For each stake $L\in\{\$1,\$10,\infty\}$ we compute, over the $12$ assignments, the one with
the lowest \emph{realised} loss --- mean bill plus $L$ times failure rate, and for
$L\to\infty$ the lowest failure rate with the bill as tie-break --- and ask whether the
rule's pick, formed from the catalog prior plus the measured cells and priced by the learned
shape, coincides with it. Figure~\ref{fig:hindsight} draws the realised set with Wilson
intervals and marks both.

It does not coincide, at any of the three stakes, and the honest account of why is
instructive.

At $L=\$1$ hindsight prefers deepseek-v4.1-flash writing with kimi-k3 reviewing (realised
loss $\$0.801$); the rule picks deepseek-v4.1-flash with qwen3.8-max-0902 (realised
$\$0.829$, $3.4\%$ worse). The same rule priced with the fixed shape picks
muse-spark-1.3 with qwen3.8-max-0902 (realised $\$1.263$, $57.5\%$ worse). At $L=\$10$ the
rule's pick is unchanged and now costs $92.1\%$ more than hindsight, while the fixed-shape
variant is unchanged at $57.5\%$ --- the fixed shape is better here, by luck, and we say so.
At $L\to\infty$ hindsight and the rule disagree again, hindsight taking the assignment with
$0/11$ failures and the rule the one with $0/4$.

The reason the rule misses the hindsight winner is not noise in the bill; it is
Assumption~\ref{ass:add}. Marginally over the ledger, kimi-k3 in the reviewing role fails on
$6$ of $31$ runs ($0.194$) while qwen3.8-max-0902 fails on $7$ of $59$ ($0.119$), so an
additive model prefers the latter at that role, correctly given its assumptions. The
hindsight winner is a \emph{pair}: kimi-k3 fails on $0$ of $11$ runs specifically alongside
deepseek-v4.1-flash. An additive logit cannot represent that, and the pair term of
Section~\ref{sec:quality} is exactly what would.

It should also be said plainly that the sample cannot resolve the difference. The hindsight
winner's failure rate is $0/11$, Wilson interval $[0.000,0.259]$; the rule's pick is $3/38$,
interval $[0.027,0.208]$. The intervals overlap over most of their length. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}, separating two models at one role by $\Delta=0.10$ at
$\kappa=30$ needs $114$ graded runs \emph{per model per role}; this ledger has $136$ runs in
total. At $L=\$1$ the bill term dominates and the rule is within a few cents of hindsight;
at $L=\$10$ the failure term dominates and the ledger has nothing to say. That transition,
rather than either number, is the finding.

\subsection{E3: does the rule converge?}

We replay the $136$ graded runs in completion order. At step $t$ the rule refits the quality
model on the runs seen so far and picks among the assignments observed so far; it is charged
the plug-in realised loss of the assignment it picks, and its \emph{empirical regret} is the
running excess over the best assignment in hindsight. Three rules are replayed: the proposed
one; B1, which is the same quality model priced by the fixed cost shape; and B0, the
scale-free knee on the fixed cost shape and the catalog score, which takes no stake and
therefore draws the same picks at every $L$. Figure~\ref{fig:converge} shows the curves and
Table~\ref{tab:e3} the slopes.

This is a replay, not a policy evaluation. The rule learns from the logged stream but does
not control it, and it is scored against full-sample plug-in means; the quantity it measures
is how fast the estimator converges to the hindsight-optimal assignment, not the regret of a
policy that also chooses its own data. Section~\ref{sec:eval} says why the stronger claim is
not available from these logs.

\emph{At $L=\$1$ the rule converges and the baselines do not.} Its per-task gap falls from
$\$0.90$ to $\$0.40$ to $\$0.027$ across the three thirds --- by the end it is within three
cents per task of the assignment hindsight would have chosen, which is the $3.4\%$ of E2
seen as a rate. B1's gap is flat at $\$0.45$: with the same quality model and the same
information, pricing by a fixed shape stops the rule converging at all. B0's is flat at
$\$0.18$ by construction, since after the front stabilises it holds one assignment forever.
The proposed rule's \emph{cumulative} regret is still the larger of the three at the end of
the replay, because of what it paid while learning; extrapolating the last third's slopes,
it overtakes B0 cumulatively after about $200$ further tasks. We report that extrapolation
as an extrapolation.

\emph{At $L=\$10$ it does not converge.} The rule's last-third slope is $\$0.58$ per task,
higher than its second third. The cause is E2's: at a stake of ten dollars the objective is
dominated by a failure-rate difference of a few percentage points between two assignments
whose Wilson intervals overlap, and $136$ runs cannot order them. The rule is not
oscillating --- it settles on one assignment and holds it --- it has settled on the wrong
one and has no evidence that would move it. This is the regime Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}
describes and it is the argument for pooling across sites, not for a different rule.

\emph{At $L\to\infty$ the experiment is uninformative.} All three rules incur $1.4$ excess
failures over $136$ runs against a hindsight optimum of zero, and all three curves are flat
after the sixth run. With three assignments at exactly zero observed failures out of
$11$, $5$ and $4$ runs, there is nothing here to distinguish. We show the panel rather than
omit it.

\subsection{What the three experiments jointly support, and what they do not}

They support the cost half of the thesis strongly and the quality half weakly, and the
asymmetry is a function of how much data each half needs. A bill is observed exactly on
every run, so $132$ runs are enough to fit a per-role token shape and to show that doing so
cuts prediction error by a factor of five and reorders the assignments. A grade is one bit
per run, so $136$ runs are nowhere near enough to order twelve assignments by failure rate,
and at a stake where the failure term dominates the rule inherits that ignorance. The
practical reading is that a deployment should expect the cost model to pay for itself
immediately and the quality model to pay for itself only with pooled evidence --- which is
what Section~\ref{sec:federation} exists for, and what Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}
predicted before any of this was measured.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{The bar.}
A rule is better if it picks assignments that are better on the cost--quality front, per
role and per task, than the posterior-mean blend of B1, on the same data. Not: if it is more
principled. Not: if it explores.

\paragraph{Off-policy estimation needs propensities that do not exist.}
For a logged history $\{(x_t,a_t,g_t)\}$ under a behaviour policy $\pi_0$ and a candidate
$\pi$, the inverse-propensity estimator
$\hat V_{\mathrm{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac1T\sum_t \frac{\pi(a_t\mid x_t)}{\pi_0(a_t\mid x_t)}\,y_t$
and its doubly robust refinement~\cite{dudik2011} both require $\pi_0(a_t\mid x_t)$ to be
recorded at the time of the pick. It is not recorded, and the reason is structural: the
assignment pick is deterministic, so $\pi_0$ is a point mass and every importance weight is
$0$ or $\infty$. \emph{So the first requirement of this protocol is a logging change}:
under the Thompson setting, record the sampled $\tilde\theta$'s seed and the resulting
selection probability (estimable by replaying the draw $R$ times at the stored posterior),
and under the greedy setting record that the propensity is one, which makes the honest
statement that no off-policy claim is possible from greedy logs.

\paragraph{Until then: replay and simulation, both labelled.}
Two admissible claims remain, and Section~\ref{sec:exp} makes exactly those. (a)
\emph{Replay}: recompute what each rule would have picked for each historical task and
compare the picks' \emph{bills} exactly --- bills are deterministic given the catalog and
the shape, so this is an exact statement about cost with no counterfactual outcome in it ---
and score the quality term against plug-in means, labelled as such. (b)
\emph{Simulation}, calibrated on the ledger, labelled as simulation in every caption, with
its sensitivity to $L$, to the prior's error and to the grade's variance reported, as in
Section~\ref{sec:decide}. Neither is a randomised trial. A randomised trial over assignments
is the right experiment and is the thing to build next; with $\Delta=0.20$ and $\kappa=30$ it
needs about six graded runs per model per role (Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}), which is
within reach of a single team over a week.

\section{Exposing the dial}
\label{sec:product}

The dial is one sentence: \emph{what does it cost you when a task comes back wrong?} A
hobby script, nothing much. A change a colleague will review, rather more. A change going
near a release, whatever it takes.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{p{2.2cm}p{2.2cm}p{9.4cm}}\toprule
preset & $L$ & what it means\\\midrule
\emph{frugal} & \$1 & a failed task is an annoyance; spend nothing to avoid one\\
\emph{balanced} & \$10 & a failed task costs about ten minutes of a person\\
\emph{max} & $\to\infty$ & the best assignment on the front, whatever the bill\\
\emph{custom} & any $L$ & the number in dollars\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two of the presets are stakes in dollars; the third is the limit. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:sweep} the limit is well defined and cheap: as $L\to\infty$ the argmin
of $J$ is the last hull vertex $a_K$, the assignment with the lowest failure probability,
with the bill as tie-break. That is what a superlative should mean. Naming a large finite
number instead --- fifty dollars, say --- would be a fourth ordinary stake wearing a
superlative's label, and on any front whose top breakpoint $L_K$ exceeds it, it would
silently decline to buy the best assignment available.

Three properties follow from the machinery rather than from the copy.

\emph{Bills become predictable per band.} Because the shortlist keeps at most $k$ models per
band (Section~\ref{sec:bands}) and the bill is computed from a measured token shape
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}), the rule can state, before the run, the band it will run in and
therefore the bill's range within a factor of $\beta=2$. A rule with a fixed shape can state
a bill computed from a constant, which Section~\ref{sec:exp} shows is wrong by a median
factor of $3.3$ at the level of a run and $2.2$ at the level of an assignment.

\emph{Every pick has a reason, and it is the posterior.} ``Why this assignment?'' is
answered by the two numbers in Eq.~\eqref{eq:J} and one from Eq.~\eqref{eq:breakpoints}:
this assignment costs \$$x$ and accepts $p$ of the time; the next one up costs \$$y$ more
and accepts $p'$; at your stake of \$$L$ the upgrade is worth it above \$$L_j$ and you are
below it. That sentence is a by-product of the posterior, not a separate explanation
feature, and it cannot drift out of agreement with the pick because it is computed from the
same numbers.

\emph{What exploration costs is a number the practitioner set.} $E$ is dollars per day. The
system never spends more than $E$ above what the greedy pick would have cost, and it reports
how much of $E$ it used.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{One repository, one language, one window.} All $202$ runs come from a single
deployment, in one repository, inside a single $21$-hour window. There are no external
sites. Every measured number in this paper --- the role shares, the proxy reliabilities, the
cost spread, all of Section~\ref{sec:exp} --- is a statement about that sample and about
nothing else. The between-site variance $\sigma_u^2$, on which the cap of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cap} entirely depends, is unmeasured, because there is exactly one site.
\item \textbf{The outcome measured is a proxy, not the grade.} The historical runs predate
an executable grader, so E2 and E3 use the reviewing role's major-defect flag (with an empty
artefact counted as a failure) in place of Definition~\ref{def:grade}. That proxy is
endogenous in exactly the way Section~\ref{sec:cascade} warns about --- the reviewing role
is part of the assignment being scored --- and Section~\ref{sec:reward} measured that a
related proxy is worth $\rho=0.42$ of a grade. Every quality claim in
Section~\ref{sec:exp} should be read through that factor. Repeating E2 and E3 against
Definition~\ref{def:grade} is the single highest-value next measurement.
\item \textbf{$g$ is binary and only as good as the checks.} A change that builds, passes
and is formatted, but does not do what the brief asked, scores $1$. A repository with weak
tests grades weakly. We regard this as the dominant threat to validity of the reward
itself: it is honest and narrow, and the narrowness is not free. Extending $g$ to a graded
scale (fraction of acceptance criteria met) is straightforward statistically and hard to
specify in a way that no model can influence.
\item \textbf{The objective is risk-neutral.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:J} optimises an expectation. A
practitioner who cares about the worst task of the week wants a mean-variance or CVaR
objective; $J_\eta(a)=\E[B]+L(1-P)+\eta\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[B]}$ is a one-line change
with a second dial, and a second dial is exactly what Section~\ref{sec:decision} argued
against. We have not resolved this.
\item \textbf{The additive logit ignores interactions, and E2 shows it costing something.}
The hindsight winner at every stake is a role pair the additive model cannot represent.
The cascade captures the largest interaction we expect structurally; a pair term would
capture more, at a cost in observations that Proposition~\ref{prop:samples} says this ledger
cannot afford.
\item \textbf{E3 is a replay, not a policy evaluation.} The rule learns from a stream it
did not choose and is scored against plug-in means. It measures estimator convergence, not
policy regret, and the two coincide only if the logged design is close to uniform over the
assignments --- which it approximately is here, by construction of the logging experiment,
but which we have not tested.
\item \textbf{$k=3$, $\beta=2$, $\lambda=4$, $h$ are judgements.} They are marked as such
in Table~\ref{tab:const} and each should be replaced by an estimate. We report the pick's
sensitivity to $\lambda$ and $\kappa$ in the released results: the picks at $L=\$1$ and
$L=\$10$ are unchanged over $\lambda\in\{0,2,4,8\}$ and over $\kappa\in\{10,30,100\}$,
while the pick at $L\to\infty$ moves at $\lambda=0$ and again at $\kappa=100$ --- which is
what one should expect of the stake at which the bill stops mattering and only the prior's
ordering of rarely-run assignments is left.
\item \textbf{Exploration was not shown to pay.} Section~\ref{sec:decide} reports a
negative result. We have not shown that Thompson sampling inside $J$ improves the rule in
the regime it will mostly run in; we have shown where it is indispensable and where it is a
tax, and set the default accordingly.
\item \textbf{No off-policy evidence is possible from these logs}
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}), and none is claimed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The decision-theoretic core is bi-objective scalarisation and the classical fact that linear
scalarisation reaches exactly the supported efficient set~\cite{geoffrion1968,ehrgott2005};
Pareto bandits~\cite{drugan2013} address the case where no scalarisation is available, which
is the case we deliberately leave by putting the exchange rate in dollars. The learner is a
combinatorial bandit with semi-bandit feedback~\cite{chen2013,wen2015,wangchen2018} under
Thompson sampling~\cite{thompson1933,russo2014,russo2018}; information-directed
sampling~\cite{russo2014ids,russo2018ids} is the value-of-information alternative discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:decide}. The budget is the bandits-with-knapsacks
constraint~\cite{bwk2013} without its algorithms. Non-stationarity is handled by variance
inflation in the manner of Glicko-2~\cite{glickman2001} and the local-level
model~\cite{harvey1989}, rather than by the discounting and sliding windows
of~\cite{garivier2011}. Switching costs and their $T^{2/3}$ consequence are Dekel et
al.~\cite{dekel2014}; anytime-valid testing at every boundary is Howard et
al.~\cite{howard2021}. Cascades over price tiers for language models are
FrugalGPT~\cite{frugalgpt2023}. Rater calibration is many-facet Rasch~\cite{linacre1989} on
the continuous scale and Dawid--Skene~\cite{dawid1979} on the binary; shrinkage is
Efron--Morris~\cite{efron1975} and robustness is Huber~\cite{huber1964}. Merging without a
coordinator is the state-based CRDT~\cite{shapiro2011}. Distributed and federated
bandits~\cite{hillel2013,shi2021} describe the coordinated exploration we do not yet do.
Off-policy evaluation is Dudík et al.~\cite{dudik2011}.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

One extraction script turns the private run ledger and the public catalog snapshot into five
text-free tables: one row per run, one per graded run, one per run with a per-role token and
dollar split, and the catalog's prices and indexes. It is the only script that reads a path
outside the release directory. A second script draws every figure and computes every number
quoted in Section~\ref{sec:exp} from those tables alone, and writes them to a plain-text
results file that is released beside the figures; the sensitivity sweeps over $\lambda$ and
$\kappa$ referenced in Section~\ref{sec:limits} are in it. Figures~\ref{fig:selfreport},
\ref{fig:cost}, \ref{fig:hindsight} and~\ref{fig:converge} are measured;
Figures~\ref{fig:hull}, \ref{fig:samples}, \ref{fig:drift}, \ref{fig:regret}
and~\ref{fig:cascade} are simulation or closed form and say so in their captions. Nothing in
the release carries task text, a file name, a brief or a reviewer's sentence.
