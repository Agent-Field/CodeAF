# Twenty Chapters

## I. Dawn

The morning opens like a held breath released,
light spilling slow across the sleeping hills,
and every shadow, gently, is deceased—
the river remembers how the cold instills
its patience in the stones. A heron stands
where reeds rehearse the wind's unfinished psalm;
the world, still soft, lies open in the lands
that have not yet remembered what is calm.

## II. The Threshold

We rise before the light has finished rising,
and close the gate behind us with one hand.
The house lets go of us without revising
its silence; we become a moving band
of breath and purpose in the hushed first air.
The path is only rumor to the eyes—
a fold of ground, a gate, a flight of stairs—
and every step makes rumor otherwise.

## III. The Road

We walk because the walking is the way,
not for the gate we think waits at the end.
The dust of other travelers, gone astray,
becomes the ground on which our own feet wend.
A signpost leans, its letters washed to bone;
we pass it, laughing, choosing left for change.
The road does not ask where we make our home—
it only asks that we continue, strange
and willing, into country without name.

## IV. The Fellow Travelers

By noon we are not walking as we started:
a shepherd joined us at the second mile,
a woman with a lantern, broken-hearted
and laughing at the same time, all the while
carrying bread enough for everyone.
We do not ask what country any lost;
the road has made a country of its own,
and every stranger pays for bread in trust.

## V. The Village

The village comes upon us like a memory
of someplace we have never lived before:
low doors, a well whose bucket turns slowly,
chickens inscribing circles on the floor
of dust outside the smithy. Someone waves.
We trade a song for supper and a bed,
and learn the names of all the local graves,
who lie as gently as the wheat lies, spread.

## VI. The Ferry

The ferry waits where the wide water narrows,
the ferryman as old as either shore.
He takes no coin but stories, and his barrows
of silence fill with ours. We gave him four:
the lost dog, the false spring, the buried ring,
the letter never sent. He nods at each,
and when he speaks, he does not say a thing
the river has not already taught to speech.

## VII. The Map

We spread the map the ferryman once drew
on paper made of bark, and read the land
it promises: a mountain white with dew,
a valley with a furnace, and a strand
of coast that runs like handwriting off the edge.
But maps are only mirrors of the walker—
the road ahead is written in the legs,
and every map is footnotes to our talk.

## VIII. The High Pass

The mountain does not hate us, though it seems
to, when the wind arrives with knives for teeth.
Above the tree line nothing moves but dreams
of weather, and our breath, and what's beneath:
a century of snow that holds the bones
of every traveler who ever tried.
We cross because the pass is where the stones
have always let the patient ones inside.

## IX. The Herd Boy

A herd boy tends his goats above the snow line,
his whistle threading through the thin blue air.
He has a face the mountains keep benign and
unreadable, and dirt-brown feet, and hair
the color of the frost. He walks with us
three valleys and a night, then turns aside
toward a track no map admits, and thus
goes home the way the water goes, denied.

## X. Storm

Then the sky folds its hands and dark descends,
rain writing shorthand on the hurried leaves.
The oak that stood a hundred years now bends
and holds—while all that hurried, quickly grieves.
We shelter in the hollow of a stone
and watch the lightning sign its one bright word,
then strike it out. What thunder overthrown
has taught the quiet: listen, and be heard.

## XI. The Aftermath

The storm has passed, and left the world rinsed clean,
the ditch a river, and the river brown.
The oak has lost a limb; between the green
and fallen, the light comes down. The town
we left behind us sends its rooster, late,
across the valley. We walk out of the stone
and shake the water off, and evaluate
the road it washed: the same, and yet our own.

## XII. The Letters

At the post road's crossing, letters wait for us,
carried three hundred miles by hands unknown.
A mother's hand, a friend's, the old addressed
in ink that fades the moment it is read.
We read them by the mile-marker's rust
and fold them, and we carry them along—
for what is written home is written thus:
that we are walking, and the walking's strong.

## XIII. The Forest

The forest takes the road and keeps it, moss
confusing every compass in the pack.
The trees go up like columns, and across
the canopy the light comes down in black
and gold. We follow water, for it knows
the shortest way, if not the fastest, out.
An owl keeps our account; the evening grows
a ceiling; and the stars put fire about.

## XIV. The Hermit

A hermit lives where three woods interlock,
in a house of drifted leaves and stacked-up stone.
He offers tea made of the pine and rock
and does not ask us anything. Alone,
he seems the most companionable man
we ever broke bread with. At dawn he stands
and points one finger down the only plan
the forest ever honored: where it lands.

## XV. The First Salt

Before we see the sea, we taste the air—
a brightness on the tongue like struck flint, brine.
The pines give up; the gorse takes over; where
the land runs out of nerve, the dunes begin.
We climb the last ridge slowly, and there spread
the whole unhurried silver of the tide.
We stand and look until the light turns red,
and something in the chest unscrews, wide-eyed.

## XVI. The Sea

At last the land gives up its argument
and drops us where the water takes the sky.
The salt wind reads the letters that we sent
to no one, and it does not ask us why.
Waves come the way that hours come—one wave
indistinguishable from all the rest,
except the one that carries us, and gave
us farther out than we had ever guessed.

## XVII. The Harbor

The harbor takes us in the way a hand
takes in a thing it meant to hold all day.
The fishing boats knock gently where they stand;
the gulls argue theology all day
in voices like the rust on old church bells.
We walk the mole as evening comes apart,
and every lantern on the quay foretells
a home we have not reached, but keep in heart.

## XVIII. The Islands

We island-hop for days: small stony worlds,
each with its chapel, well, and one child
who rows between them, fearless, hair unfurled,
ferrying news and eggs. The water, mild
between the islands, holds the sky so still
we seem to row across the underside
of heaven. On the last isle, on the hill,
a beacon heap is stacked, unlit, and dried.

## XIX. The Turning

The wind comes round at last into the west,
and every leaf on every coast-line tree
turns over like a page. We know the best
of what we carried out is what we see
now looking back: the pass, the ferry, the friend,
the hermit's tea, the islands and the boy.
The road home is the road out, to the end
and back; the difference is in the joy.

## XX. Return

And coming home, we find the door unchanged,
the kettle where it always was, the chair
still holding the warm shape of what we arranged
before we left. The year has turned its stair.
But something in us keeps the storm, the road,
the sea's long patience folded in the chest—
so that the smallest room, the lightest load,
holds all the distances we brought back, blessed.
